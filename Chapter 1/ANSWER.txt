1.8 Explain the following terms and concepts using a wristwatch as an example.
1. Object

A wristwatch is an object because it is a specific item with properties and functions. For example, a particular digital wristwatch is an object.

2. Attributes

Attributes are the characteristics or properties of an object. A wristwatch's attributes include its color, size, weight, brand, display type, and strap material.

3. Behaviors

Behaviors are the actions an object can perform. A wristwatch can display the time, measure elapsed time, and, if it has an alarm, produce an alarm sound.

4. Class

A class is a blueprint for creating objects. For example, the Watch class defines common properties and behaviors that individual wristwatches possess.

5. Inheritance

Inheritance allows a new class to acquire the properties and behaviors of an existing class. For example, an AlarmWatch class can inherit from a Watch class and add an alarm feature.

6. Modeling

Modeling is the process of representing a real-world object or system in a simplified form. A wristwatch model might show its attributes, such as time and date, and behaviors, such as displaying time and setting an alarm.

7. Messages

Messages are communications between objects that request actions or provide information. For example, a user can send a command to a digital watch to set the time or activate an alarm.

8. Encapsulation

Encapsulation combines an object's data and the methods that operate on that data into a single unit. In a wristwatch, the internal mechanisms and the methods controlling them work together as one unit.

9. Interface

An interface is the means through which a user interacts with an object. A wristwatch's buttons, crown, and touchscreen allow users to set the time, change settings, and activate features.

10. Information Hiding

Information hiding means keeping an object's internal implementation details hidden from users. For example, a person can read the time displayed on a wristwatch without knowing how its internal electronic circuits or mechanical gears work.


Research: Formulas for Calculating Carbon Footprints
1. What Is a Carbon Footprint?

A carbon footprint is the total amount of greenhouse gases released into the atmosphere as a result of the activities of an individual, organization, product, or household. It is usually measured in kilograms or tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e).

Carbon footprints include emissions from activities such as driving vehicles, using electricity, cooking with fossil fuels, air travel, and waste disposal.

2. Formulas for Calculating Carbon Footprints

The general formula for calculating a carbon footprint is:

Carbon Footprint = Activity Data × Emission Factor

Where:

Activity data represents the amount of fuel consumed, electricity used, or distance travelled.
Emission factor represents the amount of greenhouse gas emitted per unit of activity.
A. Transportation

For a petrol-powered vehicle:

Carbon Emissions (kg CO₂) = Fuel Consumed (litres) × Emission Factor (kg CO₂/litre)

For example, if a car consumes 100 litres of petrol and the emission factor is approximately 2.31 kg CO₂ per litre:

Carbon emissions = 100 × 2.31

Carbon emissions = 231 kg CO₂

B. Electricity Consumption

The formula for calculating emissions from electricity consumption is:

Carbon Emissions (kg CO₂) = Electricity Used (kWh) × Electricity Emission Factor (kg CO₂/kWh)

For example, if a household consumes 200 kWh of electricity and the emission factor is 0.5 kg CO₂ per kWh:

Carbon emissions = 200 × 0.5

Carbon emissions = 100 kg CO₂

3. Calculating Total Carbon Footprint

An individual's total carbon footprint can be estimated by adding emissions from all relevant activities:

Total Carbon Footprint = Transportation Emissions + Electricity Emissions + Heating and Cooking Emissions + Other Emissions

The result can be expressed in kilograms or tonnes of CO₂e per year.

To convert kilograms to tonnes:

Tonnes of CO₂e = Kilograms of CO₂e ÷ 1,000

4. Factors Considered by Carbon-Footprint Calculators

Web-based carbon-footprint calculators commonly consider:

Home energy: Electricity, natural gas, and other fuels used at home.
Transportation: Fuel consumption, vehicle type, and distance travelled.
Air travel: Number and distance of flights taken.
Waste: The quantity and type of waste generated.
Lifestyle: Other activities and consumption patterns that contribute to greenhouse gas emissions.

The calculators use these details and appropriate emission factors to estimate an individual's annual carbon footprint.

5. Websites for Further Research

The following websites provide carbon-footprint calculators and related information:

TerraPass: TerraPass Carbon Footprint Calculator
Carbon Footprint: Carbon Footprint Calculator
United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA): EPA Greenhouse Gas Equivalencies Calculator
Greenhouse Gas Protocol: GHG Protocol
6. Conclusion

Carbon-footprint calculators estimate the greenhouse gas emissions associated with an individual's daily activities. The basic calculation involves multiplying activity data by the appropriate emission factor and adding the results for different activities.

Understanding these formulas provides a foundation for developing a carbon-footprint calculator using a programming language such as Java. Such a program can collect information about fuel consumption, electricity usage, and transportation, calculate the resulting emissions, and display the user's estimated annual carbon footprint.
